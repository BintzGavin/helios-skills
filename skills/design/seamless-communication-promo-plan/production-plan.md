A Promo Video for a Seamless Communication Platform
A complete production plan, from blank page to shipped file.

Phase 0 — Interrogating the Brief
Before any phase that produces artifacts, I sit with the brief. "Seamless, effortless connection" is a beautiful phrase and a treacherous one — every communication platform claims it. So my first job is to translate the abstraction into something I can actually animate.

I'd want to know:

What does seamless mean to this product, specifically? Cross-device handoff? No setup friction? Messages that don't get lost? Speech-to-text? The metaphor changes entirely depending on the answer.
Who is the protagonist? A specific person with a specific need (Maya is a freelance designer; she just got a new client) is animatable. "Modern professionals" is not.
What is the opposite of seamless that we are quietly contrasting against? Effortless only means something next to effort. The video needs a faint shadow of the old way.
Where does this play? A 60s YouTube pre-roll permits a slow build. A 15s Instagram Reel demands the payoff in the first two seconds.
Brand assets? Logo, type, palette, motion guidelines. If they don't exist, we're inventing them — and that's a separate conversation.
I'd ship one paragraph back to the stakeholder before moving on: "Here's what I think we're making. Confirm or correct before I spend a day on it."

Phase 1 — Conceptualization & Storyboarding
The story, in one sentence
A thought leaves one person and arrives, intact and instant, in another — across distance, devices, and the small frictions of life.

That's the spine. Everything serves it.

The arc
I'd structure 45 seconds (the sweet spot for the brief — long enough to breathe, short enough to land everywhere) like this:

Beat	Time	Function
Cold open	0–4s	A single moment of human intent. A person thinks of someone.
Spark	4–8s	The thought becomes a message. Establish the protagonist of the visual language.
Journey	8–28s	The message travels. This is the body. Distance, devices, contexts.
Arrival	28–36s	It lands. A second person. A reaction. A reply, just as easy.
The web	36–42s	Pull back. Two becomes many. The world is connected.
Brand	42–45s	Logo. Tagline. Hold. End.
Key scenes & visual metaphors
The central metaphor I'd commit to: the message as a single point of light — a small, glowing token that we follow from sender to receiver. It's a deliberate cliché, used carefully. Clichés are clichés because they work; the craft is in the execution, not the avoidance.

Specific scenes:

The intent. Close on a face — or, if budget forbids, on hands resting on a phone, a coffee cooling, ambient afternoon light. The person's expression shifts: I should tell her. Two seconds. No UI yet.

The compose. We push into the device. The interface assembles itself around the action — not the other way around. The point: with this product, the UI doesn't precede the intent, it responds to it. Text appears as if dictated, the send action is a gesture, not a button.

The journey. This is where I'd spend the most time. The light-token leaves the device and travels through real space: across a desk, out a window, over a city skyline, through weather, down into a subway, across an ocean rendered as gentle topographic lines. The world is stylized, not literal — closer to the Spike Jonze "Her" palette than a Google Earth flyover. The token never stops moving. It threads through environments that progressively shift hue, signaling distance.

The arrival. The token lands on a second device, in a second context — maybe a kitchen at dawn (timezone shift implied). The recipient looks up, smiles. The reply happens almost without thought — voice, a glance, a gesture. Effortless is shown by the absence of the actions we'd normally see: no typing, no tapping, no waiting.

The web. Pull back. The two points become a constellation. Hundreds of light-tokens crossing between hundreds of points. The world as a soft, glowing lattice. This is what the platform looks like at scale. Held for two seconds.

The mark. Logo resolves out of the constellation — a final token settling into the brand's wordmark. Tagline beneath. Hold 2.5 seconds. End on still.

What I'd deliberately not include
No feature callouts. ("End-to-end encryption!" "1GB file transfer!") The moment a label appears, the spell breaks.
No competitor reference, even implied. The contrast with "the old way" should be felt in pacing, not stated.
No human dialogue. Voiceover, maybe. Spoken lines between characters, no.
No emoji. No emoji-as-visual-language. Ever.
Storyboard fidelity
I'd produce the storyboard at three escalating fidelities:

Thumbnail sketches (paper, 90 minutes): six panels per beat, just to feel the rhythm.
Animatic (After Effects or, in this environment, an HTML page with timed black-and-white blocks): the full 45 seconds with placeholder shapes and stopwatch timing. This is where pacing gets locked. It is faster to fix pacing in the animatic than at any later stage.
Style frame (one or two only): fully designed stills of the most important moments — usually the journey midpoint and the constellation pullback. These resolve the visual language for the rest of the team.
Phase 2 — Visual Style & Aesthetic Guidelines
Guiding principles
Before any swatch or font, three rules:

Restraint signals quality. Three colors, two typefaces, one accent gesture repeated. Less surface area = more polish per square inch.
The frame must be alive without being busy. Quiet motion in every shot — drifting gradients, breathing scale — but never more than one loud thing at a time.
Every visual choice is in service of "seamless." If an element introduces friction (a hard edge where a soft one would do, a literal UI when a gestural one would suffice, a color collision), it loses.
Color
A palette that breathes across the journey:

Foundation: an off-white near-paper (oklch(98% 0.005 80)) and a deep ink that isn't pure black (oklch(18% 0.01 260)). Warm whites, cool blacks — a tension that keeps the frame from feeling sterile.
Accent: a single signature hue. I'd lean toward a soft luminous cyan-green (oklch(78% 0.12 180)) — the color of the message-token, the light, the thread connecting everything. It needs to feel like light, not pigment, which means high lightness, moderate chroma, and a hue that doesn't read as corporate teal.
Atmospheric tints: the journey shots gently shift the background hue — dusk-warm at origin, dawn-cool at arrival. Same lightness band, hue rotates 60° over 20 seconds. The viewer doesn't consciously notice; they feel the distance.
No gradients as decoration. Gradients exist only as atmosphere — the sky, the depth of a scene — never as button fills or text treatments.

Typography
Two faces:

A humanist sans for everything. Inter is overused; I'd reach for Söhne, Untitled Sans, or General Sans. Geometric enough to feel modern, humanist enough to feel warm. A platform about human connection cannot use a typeface that reads as machinery.
A monospace for accents only — timestamps, the tagline's punctuation, debug-style flourishes. Used sparingly to suggest that there's engineering underneath the warmth, without belaboring the point.
Type appears in three contexts: the message itself (the words the protagonist sends), the tagline at the end, and possibly a single mid-piece phrase. That's it. No third-act stat slabs. No bullet points. No.

Camera language
Yes, a 2D animation has a camera. It's the choreography of frame, scale, and crop.

Establishing shots are wide and patient. Think 35mm, shallow-feel even though there's no real lens.
Action shots push in. Following the token means tracking it, often with a slight damping lag — the camera can't quite keep up with the message, which makes the speed feel real.
The arrival uses a hard cut, not a transition. The contrast between travel and stillness is the punctuation.
The pullback is a continuous zoom out, slow and inevitable, scored to a swelling note. This is the climax of the visual language.
Texture and depth
Subtle film grain across the entire piece. A 4–6% noise overlay that you'd never notice but would miss instantly if removed. It's what separates "rendered" from "filmic."
Soft, large-radius shadows on every floating element. No drop-shadows under 20px blur. We are not making a UI mockup; we are making cinema.
Depth-of-field implied via blur. When the camera is on the token, the world behind softens. This is one of the cheapest, highest-impact tricks in motion design.
What I'd refuse
Glassmorphism. Done.
Gradient mesh blobs. Done.
3D-rendered devices floating in a void. The device is a vessel for the moment, not the moment itself.
Particle systems with no narrative purpose. Particles must serve the story, or they're noise.
Generic "tech" iconography (chains, networks, globe wireframes). The metaphor is light and travel, not infographic.
Phase 3 — Animation & Motion Design Approach
Philosophy
The video is not a UI demo. It is a short film that happens to contain a product. UI moments exist — composing the message, receiving it — but they are subordinate to the larger choreography of the token's journey.

The mix:

~25% kinetic UI — the compose moment, the reply moment. Tactile, responsive, just-enough-detail to read as the actual product.
~50% motion graphics — the token's journey, the world rendered as stylized environments, the constellation pullback.
~15% kinetic typography — the tagline reveal, possibly one mid-piece phrase if narrative needs it. Not before.
~10% held beats — the moments of stillness that make the rest land.
The easing vocabulary
I'd commit to a small set of named curves and use them with discipline:

outQuint for arrivals — anything coming to rest on screen. The deceleration is gentle and confident.
outBack (with overshoot of about 8%) for the token landing in a device, the logo settling, anything that should feel delivered with weight.
inOutSine for the camera. Camera moves are the slowest, gentlest curves in the piece — the viewer should feel them as breath, not as choreography.
spring (damping 18, stiffness 220) for the token itself when it changes direction. Organic, alive.
linear is reserved for two things only: the continuous drift of atmospheric backgrounds, and the constant onward motion of the token in flight. Everything else, never.
The discipline: the same easing for the same kind of moment, every time. When every arrival uses outQuint, the viewer learns the language of the piece. They feel the consistency before they could name it.

Specific scene choices
The intent (0–4s). A static-feeling shot that isn't actually static. The light source drifts imperceptibly. The protagonist's hand has a 0.5% scale breath. A dust mote crosses the frame. The viewer reads "still" but the frame is alive.

The compose (4–8s). The text appears as if exhaled — characters fade in word-by-word, with a 60ms stagger and a tiny upward drift on each. Not character-by-character (that's typewriter, that's the opposite of effortless). Word-by-word. The send action is a gesture: the message gathers itself into a single point of light, lifts, leaves.

The journey (8–28s). Twenty seconds is long. To keep it alive, I'd cut it into four sub-shots of 5s each, each with a different environment but a consistent token. The cuts are matched on the token's position — it leaves frame right at speed X, enters the next frame at speed X, in the same screen position. The viewer's eye never has to relocate. This is the secret to making a long sequence feel short.

The arrival (28–36s). Hard cut from full-speed motion to a held shot of the recipient's device. The token enters slowly, settles with outBack. The screen blooms warm. The recipient's reaction is shown in a single secondary action — a hand lifting toward the device, a smile we read from a quarter-frame of the face. The reply is a 1.5-second mirror of the compose, faster and warmer.

The constellation (36–42s). The most important easing decision in the piece: the pullback is slow, on inOutSine, and starts before the viewer expects it. As we pull back, new tokens appear — but never all at once. They arrive in a slightly irregular rhythm (the 80/100/90/130/100 ms cadence I mentioned earlier — chaos isn't rhythm, but neither is metronome). The constellation grows organically.

The mark (42–45s). The final token settles into the dot of the logo — or the crossbar of a letterform — at exactly 43.0s. The tagline fades in 400ms later, word-by-word. Hold for 1.6s of total stillness with only the grain alive. End.

Phase 4 — Audio & Music
Audio is at minimum half the experience, and on social platforms — where most viewers watch muted — it's both half the experience and must be designed to be optional. Both versions need to work.

Music
A custom score, or — realistically — a carefully licensed track from Musicbed, Marmoset, or Artlist. The brief I'd hand the composer or licensor:

Tempo: ~85 BPM. Slow enough to feel calm, fast enough to maintain pulse.
Instrumentation: A single sustained pad (warm synth or bowed strings), a felt-piano motif of three notes that recurs three times in the piece, and a soft pulse — heartbeat, not drum kit. No vocals. No build-drop structure.
Arc: Sparse at the cold open. The piano motif enters with the message-token. The pad swells through the journey. The constellation pullback is the emotional peak — strings, or a single reverberant note. The mark lands on a single sustained tone that fades over the hold.
Mix: Always leaves room for sound design. The music is the bed, not the foreground.
Sound design
Sparingly. A communication platform should not sound like a machine.

A soft chime when the message lifts off — felt, not heard.
A whoosh of air for the journey, but stylized as breath rather than wind.
A second, warmer chime at arrival — slightly lower pitch than the send chime, so the call-and-response is a tiny musical interval the ear catches subliminally.
Tactile UI sounds (the gestural send, the gestural reply) at very low level, with high-frequency rolloff so they feel material rather than digital.
Silence in two specific places: just before the constellation pullback (one full second), and during the final 1.6s hold on the mark.
Voiceover
I'd push back on having one. Voiceover on a "feeling" piece almost always over-explains and undercuts the visuals. If the stakeholder insists, the brief is: one line, in the last five seconds, in a warm and unhurried voice, that doesn't repeat what's on screen. Something like the tagline read aloud, only if the tagline benefits from it. Cast against type — not a corporate baritone, not a peppy startup voice. Someone who sounds like a person.

Captions
Non-negotiable. Every social platform autoplays muted. Captions are not just accessibility (though they are that, first); they are how 60% of viewers will experience the piece.

Open captions burned into a 9:16 cut for Reels/TikTok/Shorts.
Closed captions as an SRT for YouTube/web.
Captions cover any voiceover, and describe meaningful sound design ("[soft chime]") in the accessibility track.
Type for open captions in the same humanist sans, bottom-third safe zone, with a subtle dark scrim so they read against any frame.
Phase 5 — Technical Workflow & Considerations
Tools, by stage
Conceptualization: paper, then a shared document. No software helps here.
Animatic: Figma frames exported with timing, or — because this is what I have here — an HTML page with timed black blocks and the music draft underneath. The animatic in HTML has a real advantage: it scrubs.
Production: This is where it forks. The "right" answer is After Effects for full-frame motion design, with Lottie or Rive for any UI elements that need to be exact. The "actually doable in this environment" answer is HTML + the animation starter component, building the entire piece as a deterministic-time React tree with <Stage> and <Sprite>. Both are legitimate. I'd default to HTML for this brief because it gives the team scrubbing, instant iteration, and a single source of truth that the eng team can reference.
Audio: Logic Pro or Ableton for the composer; the score is delivered as stems so I can balance music vs. sound design at mix time.
Final render: if HTML, screen-record at 60fps via a deterministic capture pipeline (puppeteer or built-in tooling) to ProRes, then encode per-platform from that master. If AE, render to ProRes 422 HQ as the master, encode down.
Captions: authored by hand in an SRT, then burned for open-caption cuts.
Deliverables matrix
A single piece becomes many files:

Cut	Aspect	Length	Use
Master	16:9	45s	Website, YouTube
Vertical	9:16	45s	Reels, TikTok, Shorts
Square	1:1	45s	LinkedIn, fallback Instagram feed
Cutdown	16:9	15s	Pre-roll, paid social
Cutdown	9:16	15s	Story ads
Open-caption	9:16	45s	Muted autoplay
Loopable	1:1	6s	Banner, hero loop
The vertical and square cuts are not zoomed crops of the master. They are re-framed from the same source — repositioned compositions that work as their own pieces. This is the most-skipped step in social motion work, and it's the difference between a video that feels designed for the platform and one that feels stretched to fit it.

Compromises I'd accept
Frame rate. The master is 60fps; the social cuts are 30fps. The eye barely registers the difference at the sizes social plays at, and file sizes halve.
Color depth. 8-bit for delivery. 10-bit only matters for the master archival.
Subtle motion gets lost on small screens. That 0.5% breath on the protagonist's hand will be invisible on a thumb-sized phone. I'd boost it to 1.5% in the vertical cuts. The principle holds; the execution adjusts to medium.
Length. If the data shows view-throughs collapse at 30s on a given platform, the 15s cutdown becomes the primary asset and the 45s becomes the YouTube-only piece. I'd argue against making the 45s shorter — at some point, you've cut so much that you're left with a sizzle reel rather than a story.
Compromises I'd refuse
Compressing past visible degradation to hit a file-size target. If the cyan-green token starts banding because the bitrate is too low, the entire metaphor of light is dead. I'd cut length before I'd cut quality.
Dropping the held beats to fit a shorter time. The held beats are the seamlessness. A version without them is a different video.
Adding feature callouts late in the process because marketing wants the "encryption" beat in there. The piece is a feeling, not a fact sheet. Those facts go in the caption, the post copy, the landing page.
Accessibility, beyond captions
Reduced-motion alternative for the website hero version. A 6s loop with most of the camera motion removed and only the token's flight retained, served via prefers-reduced-motion.
No flashing. The token's brightness is checked against WCAG flicker thresholds. Light is the metaphor, but it pulses at sub-threshold rates.
Color contrast on the open captions verified against a worst-case bright frame.
Alt text for the silent posters / OG images that the campaign generates around the video.
Phase 6 — Review, Iterate, Ship
Three review milestones, no more:

Animatic review. Pacing, story, beat structure. If it doesn't work in black-and-white blocks, no amount of polish saves it.
Style frame review. Color, type, atmosphere, the visual language of one or two key shots. Polish to ~95%; this is what every other shot will calibrate against.
First full render. Watched 30+ times by me before anyone else sees it. Then a single review with stakeholders, with explicit instruction: no tactical notes on shot 7's timing yet — react to the whole. Tactical notes come in round two.
I'd resist the temptation to iterate forever. There is a point — usually around the 4th full pass — where additional polish stops adding value and starts adding noise. The discipline is to ship at that point, not to keep tweaking because tweaking is comfortable.

Closing thought
A 45-second video about effortless connection should itself feel effortless to watch — and that effortlessness is the product of a tremendous amount of decision-making compressed out of view. Every choice above is in service of one outcome: a viewer, scrolling on their phone in a cafe somewhere, slows down for 45 seconds, feels something small and warm, and remembers the name of the platform a week later when they need to send a message that matters.

That's the whole job. Now, when you're ready, we can pick the first scene to actually animate — and I'd argue for the journey midpoint, because if the journey works, everything else is calibration around it.
